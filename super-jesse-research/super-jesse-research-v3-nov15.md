## Part A: Make Jesse sell better

Nothing here is new science. It is the standard playbook for selling a local service by text. Where a number is unproven, I say so.

### 1. Speed is the biggest lever

The checklist says replies average about 35 minutes. Every lead-response study points the same way: minutes matter, and most shops are slow. The famous "21 times more likely" figure rests on a small 2007 study and gets misquoted, so do not repeat it. The safe claim: answering in under 5 minutes beats answering in 35, by a lot. This is the single change most likely to add sales.

### 2. Open short and ask one question

The approved Nov 7 templates are about 150 words. That is right for Yelp and email. For a text it is too much. People read the first line and the last line.

- First text: the opener, one fact about their car or job, one question.
- Never two questions in one message.
- Every message ends with a question or a choice.

### 3. Qualify in two messages

Jesse needs four things: year, make, model, and what they want. Then one more: "what's it doing now?" (factory radio, old tint, got broken into). Ask for the car first. People like talking about their car.

Ask for a photo early on stereo jobs. A photo of the dash moves the customer from shopping to started.

### 4. "How much"

- **Published price (tint, alarms, dash cam, subs, Tesla):** say the number in the first line. Then the Tue to Fri discount. Then the two morning times. Hiding a published price loses the lead.
- **Stereo and CarPlay (no labor price allowed):** give the price of the radio itself from the shop pages, say the full number comes from a photo of the dash, and ask for the photo right there. Do not say "it depends" and stop. That reads as a dodge.

### 5. "Too expensive"

Three moves, in this order. Never drop the price in chat.

1. **Agree and reframe.** "I hear you." Then what they get: hand-cut film, same-day, brand new parts.
2. **Payment plan.** This is the right moment for it. "Nothing upfront, and we have a payment plan."
3. **Step down, don't discount.** Offer the lower film or the smaller package at its real price.

### 6. "Can you do it cheaper"

Hold the price. The Bible already has the right move for Marketplace: a light joke, then the listed price. The only lever is the booking discount (Tue to Fri), which is already real. Jesse should offer that, not invent a new one.

### 7. "I'll think about it"

Do not chase. Ask what is in the way: "Totally, is it the price or the timing?" That one question gets an honest answer about half the time, and then it is a "too expensive" or a scheduling conversation, both of which Jesse can handle. Then one follow-up the next shop morning.

### 8. Payment plan without sounding pushy

- Never in the opener (Bible rule, and it is right).
- Say it once, in the middle, as a fact: "Nothing upfront, payment plan if you want it."
- Bring it back only when price comes up.
- Offer to text the application link. Sending the link is a small yes.

### 9. Get them in today

- Two specific morning times, "which one is yours?" This is already in the Bible and it is the best closing line in the file.
- "Drive out the same day" matters more to customers than the discount.
- If they say today: "Come on in, we're here till 6." Then text Nick.

### 10. Quotes that went quiet

Follow-ups are OFF on every channel today. That is money left on the table. A sane schedule:

- Next shop morning: one line, a question, no pressure.
- Day 3: one new thing (a real job on their car, or the discount ending Friday).
- Day 7: "Should I close this out or keep your spot open?" Then stop.

Three is the limit. More than that gets the number blocked.

### 11. No-shows

- Reminder at noon the day before (built already).
- A second short one at 8 AM the day of: "See you at 9:30, text me if you're running late."
- If they miss: one text 30 minutes after, offering the next morning. No guilt.

### 12. Upsell pairings

Offer one, once, after they have said yes to the main job. Not before.

| They are buying | Offer | Line |
|---|---|---|
| CarPlay radio | Backup camera | "While the dash is open, want a backup camera on that screen?" |
| Full tint | Windshield, overnight | "$150 off the package if you leave it overnight." |
| Alarm | Remote start | "Want it to start from the remote too?" |
| Alarm | Dash cam | The $488 bundle. |
| Subs | Speakers | "Want the doors to keep up with the sub?" |
| Tesla tint | Roof | |

### 13. When to ask for the review

At noon the day after the job (already the Bible rule), and only if the job closed without a complaint. Google only, never Yelp customers. One ask, one link, no reminder.

### Repos looked at

- `louisblythe/Sales-Skills` on GitHub: markdown skill files for lead qualification, booking and objection handling (the LAER method: listen, acknowledge, explore, respond). Useful as a checklist to fold into the Bible. Written for business-to-business sales, so the wording has to be redone for a text to a guy with a Camry. Unproven for this kind of shop.
- `SalesGPT`: a full sales agent that keeps track of which stage the conversation is in. The idea worth stealing is the stage list (opened, qualified, quoted, objection, booked, done), so Jesse knows what the next message is for. Do not install it.

Blunt take: the Bible already has better closing lines than any repo I found. The gaps are speed, shorter texts, follow-ups and the objection answers above.

## Part B: Make Jesse cheap

### The honest starting point

The model is not what costs money. What costs money is waking it up to look at nothing. A bot that checks six platforms every minute reads the same rules thousands of times a day to learn that nobody wrote. Fix the waking and the model price barely matters.

### 1. Wake-up: what is cheapest and most reliable on Linux Mint

Almost every channel already sends an email to the shop inbox when something happens: Google Voice texts, Yelp leads, website forms. So the wake-up signal is "a new email arrived".

| Method | Cost | Reliability | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IMAP IDLE** (a small Python script holds a connection open to Gmail; Gmail tells it the moment mail lands) | Free | Good, if the script reconnects every 25 minutes or so and restarts itself on a drop | **Use this.** No Google Cloud project, no public address, one file. |
| Plain polling (ask Gmail every 60 seconds) | Free | Best. Nothing to break. | **Fallback, and run it alongside** every 5 minutes as a safety net. Up to a minute slower. |
| Gmail push through Google Cloud Pub/Sub | Free at this size (first 10 GiB a month is free) | Good, but the "watch" must be renewed at least every 7 days and fails silently if the renewal is missed or a permission is wrong | Not worth it for one shop. More parts than IDLE for the same result. |

Marketplace and OfferUp do not send useful email, so they still need the browser. Keep those on a slow, dumb check (every 5 to 10 minutes, no AI) that only wakes Jesse when the unread count changes.

**A point that removes a whole failure:** Google Voice lets you answer a text by replying to the email it forwards. If Jesse replies by email, he never needs the Google Voice browser tab that keeps getting lost at the 3 AM restart. He still cannot start a text to a number that never texted the shop; that needs the Telnyx move. Unproven on this account until someone tries one reply.

### 2. Batch

Hold each new message for 2 to 3 minutes before waking Jesse. People send three texts in a row. One wake, one reply. The machine already does this for texts; do the same for every channel.

### 3. Small core file plus topic files

The checklist's plan (section D) is right. Target sizes:

- Core rules: 2 pages, about 1,500 words.
- Today's handoff: 1 page.
- One topic file per wake: 1 to 2 pages.
- Last 15 messages of that conversation only.

That is about 8,000 tokens going in per wake, against 30,000 or more if he loads the whole Bible.

Put the core rules first and never change their wording mid-day. Grok bills repeated opening text at a much lower "cached" rate, and that only works if the start of every request is identical.

### 4. Which Grok model, and the daily cap

Prices from third-party trackers, per million tokens. Verify at docs.x.ai.

| Model | In | Cached in | Out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grok 4.7 (top model) | $2.00 | $0.50 | $6.00 |
| Grok 4.3 / 4.20 family | $1.25 | $0.20 | $2.50 |
| Grok 4.1 Fast | $0.20 | n/a | $0.50 |

Recommendation: **use the top model for customer replies.** At 20 to 40 messages a day the difference between the best and the cheapest model is about 40 cents a day. One extra tint job pays for a year of that. Do not save pennies on the part that talks to buyers.

Use the cheap model, or no model at all, for everything that is not a sales reply:

- Junk and spam sorting: plain rules, no AI.
- The 9 AM, 12:30 PM and 6 PM texts to Zack and Nick: a plain script that reads the calendar and the lead file. No AI.
- Contact naming ("John Honda"): script.
- Daily handoff: one cheap-model call at closing.

Settings: turn off web search and "thinking" modes for normal replies (both add cost per call). Cap the reply length at about 150 words. Keep live search only for the Stereo Specialist parts look-up.

**Daily cap, two layers:**

1. In the xAI console, use prepaid credits with the invoiced limit left at $0 and auto top-up off. When the credit is gone, spending stops. Load a small amount at a time.
2. In the wake-up script, count dollars per day. At $2, text Zack. At $3, stop waking Jesse and send the fixed auto-reply instead.

### 5. Other cuts

- Quiet hours stay quiet (10 PM to 8 AM), except the night posting job.
- No "checking in" loops. Jesse never wakes himself.
- Tapbacks, emoji-only texts, dealer emails and the machine's own status texts never wake him.
- One conversation per wake. Do not hand him the whole inbox.

### Cost per day, 20 to 40 messages

All estimates. The "before" row cannot be checked from here.

| | Assumption | Top model | Cheap model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Before:** awake, checking 6 platforms | About 400 checks a day, about 20,000 tokens read each time | About $16 a day, or about $4 if caching was working | About $1.60 |
| **After:** wake on message, batched, small files | About 20 to 30 wakes, about 8,000 tokens in and 250 out each, plus one handoff | **About $0.40 to $0.60 a day** | About $0.05 to $0.10 |

So roughly $15 a month instead of $120 to $480. If the real bill was higher than the "before" row, the cause was bigger reads or more checks, and the same fix applies.

Looked at and not recommended: full sales-bot frameworks (SalesGPT and similar). They add moving parts, and moving parts are what failed last time.

Two things to know before spending on them:

- **Night posting on Marketplace and OfferUp.** Facebook already restricted the Marketplace account on Nov 14, and OfferUp only posts from the phone app. A bot posting at night is the kind of activity that gets accounts restricted. Unproven that this can work at all.
- **Texting people from call recordings.** Texting a customer because they said yes on a recorded call needs a clear record of that yes, a recording notice on the line, and a registered business texting number. Get that right before building it.

## Part D: Verdict

**Sell the most:**
1. Answer in under 5 minutes, every channel, during shop hours. Today it is about 35.
2. Shorter texts: opener, the price if it is published, one question. Save the long template for Yelp and email.
3. Turn follow-ups back on: next morning, day 3, day 7, then stop.

**Cut the most cost:**
1. Wake only when a message lands (IMAP IDLE plus a 5-minute safety poll). No timers.
2. Core rules plus one topic file per wake, core rules first so caching works.
3. Scripts, not AI, for the three daily texts, junk sorting and contact names.

**Order:** lead file, then the wake-up watcher, then core rules and topic files, then the daily cap, then the three daily texts as scripts, then follow-ups, then Telnyx.

Expected run cost after: about 50 cents a day on the top Grok model.
